Field-Oriented Control
BLDC Actuator

In Progress — paused for hardware · The unit cell of a humanoid robot joint

The idea

A servo motor knows how to go to an angle. It doesn't know how to push — soft, hard, springy, or resistant on command. That's the difference between a robot that moves and a robot that can safely touch something. Field-Oriented Control (FOC) is the technique that gets you that: precise control over how much force a brushless motor delivers, not just where it points.

This project is a sensored FOC controller built on a bare STM32, targeting a small BLDC gimbal motor as a stand-in for the "unit cell" of a humanoid robot joint — eventually demoed as a programmable haptic knob you can feel push back on command.

The rule I set for myself going in: write the control algorithm myself. Libraries like SimpleFOC exist and work well, but using one would mean I never actually had to understand why any of it works. Buying the hardware is normal — an STM32 dev board, a motor, a magnetic encoder. Writing the math that turns raw sensor data into precise torque is the part that had to be mine.

Learning the physics first

Before writing a line of code, I worked through the fundamentals: why a stator's magnetic "arrow" produces maximum torque when it's held exactly 90° ahead of the rotor's magnets — and almost none when pointed straight at them. That single rule is the entire reason FOC exists: hold that 90° lead, continuously, no matter how fast the rotor is actually spinning.

The gotcha that trips almost everyone up first: a motor's rotor doesn't have just one north pole — it has several magnet pairs arranged around the shaft (seven, for this motor). One full mechanical rotation is actually seven electrical cycles from the stator's point of view. Forget to multiply by that pole-pair count, and the motor just buzzes — the stator arrow updates seven times too slowly to ever catch the rotor.

Deriving the math

The core of FOC is a change of reference frame. Three phase currents, each swinging sinusoidally as the motor spins, are almost impossible for a simple controller to hold steady — the target is always moving. The fix is the Park transform: mathematically "hop onto" a viewpoint spinning with the rotor, so those swinging currents freeze into two steady DC values — Id (wasted, non-torque-producing current, held at zero) and Iq (the actual torque knob).

I derived each transform from first principles rather than looking up the formulas — the Park and inverse-Park matrices came from reasoning through 2D rotation geometry directly (a stator axis rotated by the rotor's electrical angle is literally (cos θ, sin θ), and its perpendicular partner falls straight out of that). The Clarke transform's inverse required working through the actual algebra: substitution, clearing a √3 denominator, isolating a variable, combining like terms — by hand, checked against known test cases before it ever touched real hardware.

The last piece, a PI current controller, needed something I hadn't used before in C: pointers. A controller has to remember its running integral term between calls — but a normal function forgets everything the instant it returns, and passing a struct by value just hands the function a disposable copy. The fix is passing the address of one real, persistent struct, so the function can reach back and modify the original directly instead of a copy that vanishes.

Every transform was tested in isolation on my laptop before it ever touched the board — known inputs, hand-calculated expected outputs, round-trip checks (rotate forward, rotate back, confirm you land exactly where you started).

Bringing it to hardware

With the math proven correct on paper, the real board work started: a B-G431B-ESC1 (STM32G431 + integrated gate driver), a small gimbal motor, and an AS5600 magnetic encoder. First milestone was the simplest possible proof of life — open-loop spin, no feedback at all, just a commanded angle ramped blindly and driven straight into three-phase PWM. It worked. The motor turned.

Next came real sensing. I brought up I2C from scratch to read the AS5600's raw angle register, then hit the second classic FOC gotcha: the encoder's "zero" and the motor's true electrical zero have no relationship to each other. They're two independently-mounted physical reference points. Fixing that meant an actual calibration step — hold the motor at a known, fixed electrical angle using the open-loop drive, let the rotor physically settle into that position, then read whatever the encoder reports at that exact moment. That single number becomes a permanent offset, subtracted from every future reading for the life of that specific physical assembly.

Current sensing came next, and it was a genuinely harder peripheral bring-up than I2C — the board doesn't expose current directly on an ADC pin. It routes each phase's tiny shunt voltage through the STM32G4's internal op-amps (in PGA mode, gain of 16), which then feed an ADC channel entirely inside the chip. Getting the CubeMX configuration right took a real dig through the board's official documentation to confirm exact pin mappings — and one specific gotcha where the ADC's internal channel stayed grayed out until I found the right op-amp mode ("internally connected," not just "connected") in a tooltip.

The hardware debugging saga

This is the part that doesn't show up in a bullet point but is honestly the most real engineering work in this project.

Board #1 died first — a torn PCB pad while soldering the encoder's I2C wires, an early soldering-technique problem, not a design flaw. That one meant a full board replacement and, more usefully, forced me to actually get good at fine soldering: flux first, heat the pad (not the wire), let it wick in, anchor every wire for strain relief. That technique held up for everything after.

Board #2 got further — encoder working, current sensing bringing up real readings off real hardware — before a different failure: the motor's phase wires briefly touched each other during handling, more than once, tripping the power supply into current-limit mode each time. Eventually the board started buzzing erratically and drawing current with zero actual motor output. I diagnosed it the way you'd diagnose any hardware fault systematically: isolated the encoder wiring first (clean), then checked every pair of phase outputs for unwanted continuity (found it — all three phases shorted to each other), then checked whether that short reached ground or the supply rail (it didn't — isolated specifically phase-to-phase), then re-did the external wiring entirely to rule out a bad joint (short persisted). That combination of results pointed at something external tools couldn't fix: internal damage inside the driver chip's switching transistors, most likely from the repeated short-circuit stress.

Two boards down isn't a skill problem — the first failure was a soldering-technique gap that's since closed for good; the second is a wire-handling lesson (heat-shrink every phase connection before power, always) that makes a third attempt meaningfully lower-risk, not a repeat of the same mistake.

Where it stands

The project is honestly paused right now, waiting on a replacement board. But nothing about that pause touches the actual hard work: every transform, the PI controller, the encoder calibration logic, and the current-sensing bring-up code are all done, tested, and sitting in the repo — ready to reflash onto new hardware with essentially zero rework. What's left is finishing current-sensing verification under real load, wiring the math into a proper timer-interrupt-driven control loop (replacing the current polling-based test code), and then closing the loop for real torque control.

The impedance-control demo — programmable stiffness and damping, the actual haptic-knob payoff — is the stretch goal once torque control is solid.
