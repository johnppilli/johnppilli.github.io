Field-Oriented Control
BLDC Actuator

In Progress (paused for hardware) · The unit cell of a humanoid robot joint

The idea

Field-Oriented Control lets you set how much torque a brushless motor puts out, not just where it points. I'm running a sensored FOC controller on a bare STM32, driving a small BLDC gimbal motor. The long-term goal is a "unit cell" for a humanoid robot joint. I wrote the control math myself instead of using an existing library like SimpleFOC, since the point was understanding it, not just using it.

Deriving the math

FOC works by changing reference frames: the Clarke transform collapses three swinging phase currents into two, then the Park transform rotates onto the spinning rotor's frame, turning those into two steady values — Id (held at zero) and Iq (the actual torque knob) — that a PI controller can regulate directly. I derived Clarke, Park, inverse-Park, and the PI controller myself and verified each one by hand-calculated test cases before any of it touched the board.

Bringing it to hardware

Running on a B-G431B-ESC1 (STM32G431 + integrated gate driver) with an AS5600 magnetic encoder. Open-loop spin worked first. Then I brought up I2C to read the encoder, added an offset calibration to align its "zero" with the motor's true electrical zero, and brought up current sensing through the STM32G4's internal op-amps (PGA mode) feeding an internal ADC channel.

Hardware failures

Lost two boards along the way. The first died to a torn PCB pad from an early soldering mistake — fixed by actually learning proper technique (flux first, heat the pad not the wire, anchor for strain relief). The second died to a shorted driver IC after the motor's phase wires touched during handling one too many times — diagnosed by continuity-testing every phase pair and ruling out a short to ground before landing on internal driver damage. Both are wiring/handling lessons now closed for good, not repeat risks.

Where it stands

Paused while I get a replacement board. All the software — transforms, PI controller, encoder calibration, current-sensing bring-up — is done, tested, and ready to reflash. Next: verify current sensing under load, move the math into a timer-interrupt-driven control loop, then close the loop for real torque control. Stretch goal after that: impedance control — the actual haptic-knob demo.
